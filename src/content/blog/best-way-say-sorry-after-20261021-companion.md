I snapped beats you made me snap.</li></ul></div>
      <p>Ask a couple what they argued about last month and they will struggle to remember. Ask them how the argument ended and the memory is sharp. That asymmetry is the whole point. Small fights are rarely about the dishes or the phone or the text left unanswered. They are about whether the two of you can come back together quickly once something has gone slightly wrong. Repair is a skill, and like most skills it improves with practice and a few reliable sentences.</p>
      <h2>Why the first hour matters more than the argument</h2>
      <p>In the first hour after a small fight, both people are still close to the original hurt and far from the hardened position. That window is generous. A warm gesture lands easily, an apology costs almost nothing, and the argument stays small. Leave it overnight and the same exchange requires far more effort, because by morning each person has rehearsed a version of events in which they were reasonable and the other person was not. Most couples who describe themselves as bad at conflict are not bad at arguing. They are simply slow at repairing, and slowness is what turns a four-minute disagreement into a two-day mood.</p>
      <h2>The four parts of an apology that lands</h2>
      <p>There is a shape to an apology that works, and it is short enough to remember while annoyed.</p>
      <ul><li>Name the action, not the mood: I raised my voice, rather than I was stressed.</li><li>Name the impact: it made you feel like you had to defend yourself.</li><li>Drop the word but. Everything before but is heard as negotiation.</li><li>Offer one change, not a transformation: next time I will take a minute before answering.</li></ul>
      <p>That is four sentences. They cost about eight seconds and they end most silly fights on the spot, because the other person can hear that you understood what actually happened rather than what you think happened.</p>
      <div class="article-callout"><strong>A gentle rule:</strong> apologize for your part, not their reaction. I snapped beats you made me snap, every time.</div>
      <h2>Five scripts for the fights couples actually have</h2>
      <p>Adapt the wording to your own voice. The structure is what carries the repair.</p>
      <ul><li>One of you arrived late: I did not text when I saw the time, and you sat there wondering whether I cared. That was thoughtless. From now on I will send one line the moment I know.</li><li>Someone was on the phone at dinner: I picked up the phone in the middle of our evening and made you feel like the second priority. I am putting it in the other room for the rest of the night.</li><li>A forgotten errand: I said I would handle it and then I did not, which left you doing it tired. That is on me. I will put it in my calendar with an alarm instead of in my head.</li><li>A sharp tone: I answered you like you were an inconvenience. You were asking a fair question. I am sorry, and I am ready to hear the rest of it properly.</li><li>An unanswered message: I read it and did not reply, and you were left guessing what I meant. That is a horrible way to spend an afternoon. I will reply even when the reply is just give me an hour.</li></ul>
      <h2>What not to say</h2>
      <p>Three phrases undo more repairs than any other. I am sorry you feel that way apologizes for their feelings rather than your action, and both people can hear it. Everything before the word but is erased by the word but. And I already said sorry converts an apology into a debt being collected, which turns the repair into a second fight. If you have said sorry and it did not land, the useful follow-up is a question, not a reminder: what would help right now?</p>
      <h2>When an apology is not the answer</h2>
      <p>Sometimes you are not actually in the wrong, and a fake apology is worse than none, because it teaches both of you that repair is a performance. In that case, repair by acknowledgement instead. You can say: I do not think I was wrong about this, and I can see it landed badly, and I do not want it to sit between us all evening. That keeps the disagreement intact without keeping the distance intact. It is also worth separating a silly fight from a real one. Silly fights are about small moments and are best repaired fast and lightly. Real fights, about trust or money or family, deserve a slower conversation with a proper time set aside.</p>
      <h2>Practice repair while nothing is wrong</h2>
      <p>The best time to agree on a repair ritual is a calm Tuesday, not a tense Friday. Choose a phrase that means this is getting too big, let us pause, and a gesture that means I am still here. A code word, a hand squeeze, a joke that only works between you. Agreeing on it in advance removes the hardest part of repair, which is finding the words while your pride is loud. Couples who have practiced repair in peacetime reach for it faster when it counts, because it is already familiar rather than brave.</p>
      <h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
      <h3>How long should I wait before apologizing?</h3><p>For a small fight, minutes rather than hours. The longer you wait, the more the other person rehearses their case and the more effort the repair requires. If you are too angry to speak kindly, say one sentence before you go quiet: I need ten minutes and then I want to fix this.</p><h3>What if my partner never apologizes first?</h3><p>Ask for the pattern, not the apology. Something like I have noticed I am usually the one who reaches first, and I would like us to take turns changes the conversation from blame to design. Some people simply need to be told that repair is expected of them too.</p><h3>Should we talk it through or just move on?</h3><p>Do both, in that order. Repair first, so the warmth returns, then talk if the issue is recurring. Skipping the conversation leaves a pattern; skipping the repair leaves a mood. Small one-off fights usually need only the repair.</p><h3>Does apologizing first make me the weaker one?</h3><p>No, and most couples learn this eventually. Reaching first is a position of strength, because it means your mood is not being decided by somebody else's stubbornness. The person who repairs first is usually the person with more options, not fewer.</p>
