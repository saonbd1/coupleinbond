      <h2>People avoid hard talks because the cost of starting them is unclear</h2>
      <p>A difficult conversation feels unsafe for one specific and fixable reason: nobody knows how it ends. If raising a topic has previously produced a fight, a shutdown, or a promise that went nowhere, the brain files the subject as expensive and stops permitting the opening sentence. That is why two people can be devoted to each other and structurally unable to discuss one particular thing. The remedy is not more love. It is a clearer frame. When both people know the length, the order, and what may actually be decided, the subject stops costing an unknown amount. The four options in the poll above each reduce that unknown in a different way. Asking permission makes the opening mutual. A neutral time makes it low-stakes. One person speaking without answering makes it safe to be incomplete. Agreeing that nothing must be settled tonight makes it safe to stop.</p>
      <div class="article-callout"><strong>A gentle rule:</strong> raise hard topics while you are both calm, not while one of you is right.</div>
      <h2>Four frames that make a difficult conversation shorter</h2>
      <ul><li>Ask first: is now a workable time for a difficult subject</li><li>Set a limit: twenty minutes, one topic, nothing in the way</li><li>One at a time: the first person finishes before the second answers</li><li>End with a next step, however small, or say plainly that there is none yet</li></ul>
      <h2>Safety is built out of finished conversations</h2>
      <p>The first difficult talk will be clumsy, which is the argument for attempting it while the stakes are still small. Choose a cheap subject before the expensive one: the chore arrangement rather than the family problem. Every conversation that starts and ends without damage lowers the price of the next one. Couples who can discuss hard things are not couples who stopped caring about the outcome. They are couples who have evidence, collected one awkward twenty-minute talk at a time.</p>

      <h3>How often should a couple talk about the relationship?</h3><p>Less often than you might expect, but at a set time. A short weekly check-in is easier to keep than daily heavy conversations, and it stops small irritations from stacking up until they arrive all at once. Start with one item under &quot;Four frames that make a difficult conversation shorter&quot;.</p><h3>We like each other but feel flat. Is that a problem?</h3><p>It is a signal rather than a verdict. Liking someone and feeling connected to them are different things, and flatness usually means the shared-attention habit lapsed first. Repeat one item from &quot;Four frames that make a difficult conversation shorter&quot; for three weeks before you judge whether anything moved.</p><h3>Does spending more time together build connection?</h3><p>Not by itself. Two people can share a sofa for three hours and exchange nothing. Connection follows attention, so one short undistracted conversation tends to do more than a long evening spent side by side on separate screens.</p>
