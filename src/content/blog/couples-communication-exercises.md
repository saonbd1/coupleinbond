<p>When a relationship feels busy or tense, “we need to communicate better” can sound too large to act on. A small communication exercise gives the idea a shape. Start with ten minutes, remove the pressure to solve everything, and focus on understanding what is happening between you.</p><h2>1. The two-minute turn</h2><p>Set a timer for two minutes. One person answers: “What has been taking up space in my mind?” The other person listens without interrupting, correcting, or planning a response. Switch roles, then each person says one sentence beginning with “What I heard was…”</p><h2>2. Appreciation with evidence</h2><p>Instead of saying only “I appreciate you,” name the action that helped. Try: “I felt cared for when you made room for that call,” or “I noticed you remembered the detail I mentioned.” Specific appreciation is easier to trust and easier to repeat.</p><div class="article-callout"><strong>Try this before a hard conversation:</strong> agree on the goal. Do you want comfort, a decision, an apology, or simply to be heard?</div><h2>3. The repair question</h2><p>After a disagreement, ask: “What part of that moment felt hardest for you?” Listen for the feeling underneath the argument. You do not have to agree on every detail to acknowledge that the moment was painful or frightening.</p><h2>4. A weekly check-in</h2><ul><li>What felt good between us this week?</li><li>Where did we miss each other?</li><li>What do we need more of next week?</li><li>What is one small thing we can make easier?</li></ul><p>Keep the check-in short enough to repeat. If a conversation becomes unsafe, coercive, or overwhelming, pause and seek appropriate professional support. A prompt is not a substitute for care.</p>
