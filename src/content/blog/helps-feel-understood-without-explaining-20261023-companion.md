      <h2>Understanding is a habit of attention, not a talent</h2>
      <p>Some people seem easy to read and others seem impossible, and most of that difference is where attention was aimed rather than how much love was felt. A partner who notices that your tone changed at dinner is not gifted. They have simply kept a record of your ordinary baseline and can tell when it moves. That record is what makes understanding look effortless, and it is available to anybody willing to pay attention on purpose. The poll above lists the four ways people most often report feeling understood, and they share one property: each requires the other person to have noticed something first and then acted on it without being asked. This is also why being understood cannot be requested. A demand to be read is a contradiction, and the moment the explanation is required the evidence has gone.</p>
      <div class="article-callout"><strong>One honest test:</strong> name one thing about your partner that changed this month and that they never mentioned.</div>
      <h2>Four ways to prove you are paying attention</h2>
      <ul><li>Say the thing you noticed instead of waiting to be asked about it</li><li>Ask about the meeting, the neighbour, or the worry from three days ago</li><li>Match the room: company on a flat day, quiet on an overloaded one</li><li>Send one message during the day that proves they were on your mind</li></ul>
      <h2>Build the baseline you want to be read from</h2>
      <p>Understanding runs on ordinary data. The less a couple talk about nothing, the harder it becomes to notice when something is wrong, because there is no normal left to compare against. Start by trading one daily summary for one specific question. Not how was your day, which returns a shrug, but what happened with the thing you were dreading on Tuesday. The record builds quickly, and being understood gets easier in proportion to it.</p>

      <h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
      <h3>How often should a couple talk about the relationship?</h3><p>Less often than you might expect, but at a set time. A short weekly check-in is easier to keep than daily heavy conversations, and it stops small irritations from stacking up until they arrive all at once. Start with one item under &quot;Four ways to prove you are paying attention&quot;.</p><h3>What does emotional availability actually mean?</h3><p>Answering instead of deflecting, and staying present when the topic is uncomfortable. If one partner routinely leaves the conversation, availability is the thing to work on before the subject of the conversation changes at all.</p><h3>Does spending more time together build connection?</h3><p>Not by itself. Two people can share a sofa for three hours and exchange nothing. Connection follows attention, so one short undistracted conversation tends to do more than a long evening spent side by side on separate screens.</p>
