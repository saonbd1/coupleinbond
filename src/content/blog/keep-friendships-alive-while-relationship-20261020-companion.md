      <p>There is a specific, quiet moment when a friendship starts to go: the third time in a row you say you are too tired. Nobody is being cruel. You genuinely are tired, and staying in with the person you love is easier than getting on a bus. That is exactly why the loss is so hard to notice. It never looks like neglect while it is happening. It only looks like neglect two years later, when the group chat has gone silent and you are not sure whether to text first.</p>
      <h2>How friendships actually disappear</h2>
      <p>Friendships are the only close relationship with no built-in maintenance. You live with your partner. You see your family at holidays. Your friends require a deliberate plan, every single time, forever. That structure makes friendship uniquely fragile during the first two years of a relationship, which is precisely when it gets the least attention. The other detail that matters: friendship has no script for conflict. Couples argue and repair. Friends usually just drift, because nobody wants to be the one who makes it awkward by asking why you stopped calling. So the drift happens quietly, and both people feel a little rejected while privately assuming the other one lost interest.</p>
      <h2>Three circles, not one</h2>
      <p>Couples do better when they stop treating friends as a single joint category. There are three circles, and each needs its own maintenance.</p>
      <ul><li>My friends: people you knew first. They need time with you alone, not just with your partner present.</li><li>Your friends: people your partner knew first. These need the same, plus your genuine warmth when you do join.</li><li>Our friends: people you met together or adopted as a pair. These need plans that do not depend on one person organizing everything.</li></ul>
      <p>The most common failure is letting the third circle absorb the first two. It feels efficient and sociable, and it quietly ends the one-to-one friendships that existed before the relationship did.</p>
      <h2>Put the friendship on the calendar</h2>
      <p>Spontaneity is a poor strategy here. Spontaneous friend time competes with staying home, and staying home wins most weeks, because it is warmer and cheaper and requires no shoes. Scheduled friend time survives, because it is an appointment rather than an option. A recurring slot, once a week or once a fortnight, does more for a friendship than a hundred good intentions. It also removes the guilt from both sides: nobody has to ask, and nobody has to decline, because the slot already exists.</p>
      <div class="article-callout"><strong>A gentle rule:</strong> each partner keeps one protected night, and nobody reports back. No check-ins, no questioning the bill, no waiting up to ask how it went.</div>
      <h2>Being a good partner to their friends</h2>
      <p>You do not have to love your partner's friends. You do have to be a decent host to them. Learn the names of the people they mention repeatedly. Remember the one who had the surgery, the one going through the divorce. Ask about them occasionally. That small effort buys your partner a great deal of ease, because the worst version of this situation is a partner who feels they must choose between your comfort and their friend's presence. Surveillance is the other failure mode: texting during the evening, expecting a report on who was there, or treating a night out as something to be monitored rather than permitted.</p>
      <h2>When you do not like their friend</h2>
      <p>Dislike and harm are different, and the difference matters. Dislike is about taste: the friend is loud, or dull, or exhausting, or not your kind of person. That is a boundary-setting conversation, not a veto, and the honest version sounds like wanting fewer joint evenings rather than fewer friendships. Harm is about behavior: this person belittles your partner, encourages dishonesty, or treats people badly. That is worth naming clearly and directly, and it is the only situation where an opinion about a friend reasonably becomes a boundary about the friendship.</p>
      <h2>When friend time feels like a threat</h2>
      <p>Jealousy about friends is rarely about the friend. It is usually about exclusion, and the fear beneath it is simple: they have a part of their life that does not include me. Naming that directly is far more useful than critiquing the friend. Try something like: I notice I get anxious when you make plans without me, and I think it is about feeling left out rather than about who you are seeing. That sentence opens a conversation. An accusation about the friend closes one. It also helps to build your own circle rather than only negotiating over theirs, because a partner with a full social life has much less to feel anxious about.</p>
      <h2>The yearly friend audit</h2>
      <p>Once a year, each of you writes down the names of friends you have not seen in six months and genuinely miss. Not the ones you have drifted from on purpose. The ones you would be sad to lose. Then text one of them this week with a concrete plan rather than a vague intention, because a message that says we should catch up sometime is the beginning of the drift rather than the end of it. If you want to check how you and your partner handle closeness and space more broadly, the <a href="relationship-compatibility-quiz.html">relationship compatibility quiz</a> is a decent starting point.</p>
      <h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
      <h3>How often should you see your friends when you are in a relationship?</h3><p>There is no universal number. The useful test is whether each partner has at least one recurring plan of their own, and whether the frequency feels chosen rather than negotiated. If one person has to ask permission each time, the arrangement is already unbalanced.</p><h3>Is it normal to lose friends when you start dating someone?</h3><p>It is common, and it is not inevitable. The first year is when friendship is easiest to neglect because a new relationship consumes attention naturally. Treat one protected friend plan as non-negotiable from the beginning, and most of that loss never happens.</p><h3>Should couples share all the same friends?</h3><p>No. A fully shared social life looks tidy and tends to be brittle, because both people depend on one circle and neither has a place to talk freely. Overlapping circles with some separate friendships is more resilient over time.</p><h3>How do I tell my partner I need more time with my friends?</h3><p>Ask for the time rather than defending the friends. Something like I want to keep Thursday open for my friends, the way you keep yours is easier to hear than a comparative list of who has how much free time. Then make the plan real rather than conditional.</p>
