<h2>The loop runs on mismatched recovery speeds</h2>
<p>In a push-pull pattern, two nervous systems recover from conflict at different speeds and in different directions. One person returns to baseline through contact, so distance feels like danger and they pursue. The other returns to baseline through space, so contact during overload feels like pressure and they withdraw. Each person&#x27;s solution deepens the other&#x27;s fear, and the cycle feeds itself without either partner wanting it. Naming the pattern out loud is usually the first interruption, because the moment both people can say this is the loop instead of this is you, the argument stops being about blame. Attachment styles are tendencies rather than fixed types, and the cycle is a two-person system, not one partner&#x27;s defect.</p>
<div class="article-callout"><strong>A gentle rule:</strong> name the cycle, not the partner. We are in it again lands softer than you always do this.</div>
<h2>Four interruptions couples actually use</h2>
<ul><li>Agree on a pause length, so space is promised rather than indefinite</li><li>Say the need behind the move: I am pursuing because I feel dropped</li><li>Set a reunion time before the pause starts, however short</li><li>Debrief the loop when both of you are calm, never mid-cycle</li></ul>
<h2>Slow is a setting, not a failure</h2>
<p>Breaking a long-running loop rarely happens in one conversation. It happens when a couple survives one cycle differently, then another, and starts collecting evidence that distance no longer means abandonment and closeness no longer means capture. Expect clumsy attempts at first. The couples who get out of the pattern are not the ones who never slip back into it; they are the ones who notice sooner and name it faster each time.</p>
